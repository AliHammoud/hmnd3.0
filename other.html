Data modelling and systems architecture. Mostly pharma.

Before any of it works, your systems have to agree on what things are.

A patient. A site. A batch. A customer. Most companies have half a dozen systems that each answer differently, and everything downstream inherits the disagreement: the reports, the dashboards, the models. We sort that out first, then build on top of it.

Why these projects stall.

It usually breaks before the AI is involved.

  1. 01

    You have a data platform. What you don’t have is anything that records what the data means. A table called PATIENT tells you there are rows in it. It doesn’t tell you whether someone who withdrew and later enrolled again is one patient or two, and there are probably three teams in your company who would each answer that differently.

  2. 02

    The pilot worked because somebody hand picked the data for it. Production is a different place. Production has the twenty year old records, the acquisition that was never fully migrated, and the free text field that half a department has been using as a status flag for a decade.

  3. 03

    Ask two systems how many active studies there are and you will get two numbers. Neither is wrong, exactly. They are answering slightly different questions, and nobody ever wrote down which question was the right one. A model trained on top of that does not resolve the disagreement. It averages it, and then it sounds completely confident.

None of this is an AI problem. It is a definitions problem, and it sits underneath everything you are currently trying to fix.

How we work.

  1. 01

    Write down what is actually there.

    We start by mapping what your business really contains: the entities, the rules, and every exception your systems already encode badly and inconsistently, including the ones that three separate teams each believe they own. This takes longer than anyone wants it to. It is also the only part that cannot be skipped, because everything after it is guesswork otherwise.

  2. 02

    Design against real constraints.

    Then we design, and every decision has to survive three questions. What breaks if this is wrong. What it earns if it is right. Who actually has to use it. Anything that cannot answer all three comes back out.

  3. 03

    Prove the risky part first.

    We build whatever is most likely to fail before we build anything else, on your real data, at small scale. Better to find out in week three than in month nine. Nobody has ever been talked out of a bad architecture by a slide.

What we are not

We do not sell a platform, so there is nothing to renew and nothing to be locked into. You keep the model, the code, and the people who understand both. We do not do staff augmentation, because we are not bodies to put against a backlog. And we do not stop at a recommendation, since a recommendation nobody can build from is just an opinion with a budget attached.

If a design cannot survive an hour of questions from the engineer who has to build it, it is not a design yet.

How this starts.

Every engagement starts the same way. A mapping engagement, with the scope and the price agreed before we begin. We spend that time working out what your business actually contains and where your systems currently disagree about it, and you get back a written architecture: what to build, in what order, what it costs you if it is wrong, and what it earns if it is right. It is yours either way. If you never work with us again, you still have it.
